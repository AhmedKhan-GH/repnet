\section{Introduction}
Risk Estimation of Preeclampsia Network (RepNet) is our research in improving the machine learning and deep learning classification of 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) waveforms for estimation of preeclampsia. We are implementing a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) with experimental attention mechanisms for temporal and cross-lead analysis. This builds off of the sole previous study in CNN based ECG classification for preeclampsia by Butler et al \cite{butler_ai-based_2024}. Furthermore we are implementing a random forest classifier that extracts manually specified ECG features to explain classification results. This builds off general ideas and approaches of a previous study in ECG classification for RWMA by Joshi et al \cite{joshi_linking_2025}.

We are currently running model training experiments on architectural dimensions informed by exploratory data analysis. We are running our model training and validation on PyTorch and Scikit-Learn through the Optuna hyperparameter optimization framework which enables exploration of the parameter and architectural space of the model. Optuna uses Tree-structured Parzen Estimator to maximize training results across runs by selecting hyperparameters from a continually updated probability distribution [cite optuna paper]. This approach was inspired by Joshi et al \cite{joshi_linking_2025}.

For model refinement, we intend to perform ablation studies and dimensionality reduction on the input \cite{xue_does_2024,attia_automated_2022}, and analyze saliency maps and attention maps to determine the relevant data for classification to inform reduced-lead learning and input [Gradcam citation]. We intend to explore purpose build ECG preprocessing strategies for signal sanitization \cite{jia_preprocessing_2024,satija_review_2018} and data augmentation strategies to manage class imbalance \cite{rahman_systematic_2023,xia_generative_2023}. We intend to explore specific feature extractions of preeclampsia \cite{angeli_novel_2015,raffaelli_pre-eclampsia_2014} and explore the larger field of ECG feature analysis \cite{kaplan_berkaya_survey_2018,singh_ecg_2023}. We intend to explore past work of preeclampsia decision tree strategies \cite{park_pchd_2014,musa_predicting_2023}, to better classify and explain results with our random forest.
We intend to perform model compression for the deployment on edge devices and compute constrained environments such as Jetson Nano or Raspberry Pi \cite{keivanimehr_tinyml_2025,gupta_comprehensive_2024}.
Lastly we intend to combine our model with our in-house real-time machine learning visualizer named Caliper to demonstrate clinical workflows augmented by machine learning and deep learning, for final presentation at the UC Davis Senior Design Showcase in June 2026 [cite caliper github].

\subsection{Previous Work}

We build off of the work by Butler et al.\ and their ECG-AI which we have obtained from GitHub \cite{butler_ai-based_2024}. ECG-AI is a ResNet-based 1-D CNN that accepts 12-lead ECG signals of length 2250 samples as a channel-dependent input. The architecture comprises six residual blocks organized into three stages of progressively increasing filter width (16, 32, 64). All convolutions use kernel size 3 with L2 regularization ($\lambda = 10^{-4}$). Each block ends with max-pooling and dropout (0.1). Blocks 3 and 5 (the first block of Stages 2 and 3) additionally use strided convolutions (stride=2) with a $1\times1$ projection for the skip connection to match the increased filter count. A final flatten and dense softmax layer produces a two-class output.

\subsection{Channel Independence via Lead Correlation}

To determine whether the 12 ECG leads should be processed jointly (channel-dependent) or independently (channel-independent), we measure how much preeclampsia disrupts the inter-lead correlation structure. For each recording $n$, we compute the $12 \times 12$ Pearson correlation matrix $C^{(n)}$ across leads, then average within each class [cite Pearson correlatin]

\begin{equation}
\bar{C}_{\text{PE}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{PE}}} \sum_{n \in \text{PE}} C^{(n)}, \qquad
\bar{C}_{\text{Normal}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{Normal}}} \sum_{n \in \text{Normal}} C^{(n)}
\end{equation}

We then compute the Frobenius norm of the difference:

\begin{equation}
\| \bar{C}_{\text{PE}} - \bar{C}_{\text{Normal}} \|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{12}\sum_{j=1}^{12} \left(\bar{C}_{\text{PE},ij} - \bar{C}_{\text{Normal},ij}\right)^2} = 1.026
\end{equation}

A high Frobenius norm ($>2.0$) would indicate that preeclampsia significantly alters how leads correlate with each other, meaning the cross-lead relationship itself is a discriminative feature best captured by 2D convolution across leads. Our value of 1.026 indicates only modest disruption --- the leads correlate roughly the same way in both classes. The discriminative information therefore resides within each lead's waveform, not in inter-lead coupling, justifying per-lead 1D convolution (channel-independent processing).

ECG-AI inputs the entire $12 \times 2500$ data matrix into the CNN, where all leads mix from the first convolutional layer. RepNet instead processes each lead through its own 1D CNN stream, extracting per-lead features before combining them via cross-lead attention in subsequent stages.

\subsubsection{Kernel Size and Layer Depth for Receptive Field}
We use per-lead power spectral density to determine kernel width and how they build up towards our receptive field. For each lead $\ell$ and recording $n$, the power spectral density is estimated via Welch's method, which divides the signal $x^{(n)}_\ell(t)$ into overlapping segments, windows each with a Hann function, computes the periodogram of each segment, and averages them (The use of fast Fourier transform for the estimation of power spectra: A method based on time averaging over short, modified periodograms)

\begin{equation}
\hat{S}^{(n)}_\ell(f) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \left| \text{DFT}\left[ w(t) \cdot x^{(n)}_{\ell,k}(t) \right] \right|^2
\end{equation}

\noindent where $K$ is the number of segments, $w(t)$ is the window function, and $x^{(n)}_{\ell,k}(t)$ is the $k$-th segment. We then average across all recordings within each class to obtain class-level PSD estimates $\bar{S}_{\text{PE}}(f)$ and $\bar{S}_{\text{Normal}}(f)$, and identify the frequency $f_{\text{peak}}$ at which their absolute difference is maximized. At our sampling rate of 250\,Hz with a segment length of 512 samples, this yields a frequency resolution of approximately 0.49\,Hz.

See Appendix~\ref{appendix:psd} for the full per-lead PSD comparison. The greatest class divergence occurs at approximately 1\,Hz across all leads, indicating that the discriminative signal is concentrated at low frequencies.

To capture a feature at frequency $f$, a convolutional receptive field must span at least one full cycle of that frequency. At 250\,Hz sampling rate, one cycle of the 1\,Hz discriminative peak corresponds to 250 samples. The network's receptive field must therefore exceed 250 samples for the convolutional pathway alone to detect this feature. Rather than using a single large kernel, we stack smaller convolutions with max-pooling, where each pooling layer doubles the effective stride and thus the receptive field grows geometrically with depth. The receptive field at layer $l$ follows:

\begin{equation}
\text{RF}_l = \text{RF}_{l-1} + (k_l - 1) \times \prod_{j=1}^{l-1} s_j
\end{equation}

\noindent where $k_l$ is the kernel size and $s_j$ is the stride (including pooling) at layer $j$. Table~\ref{tab:receptive-field} compares the receptive field buildup between ECG-AI and RepNet Baseline.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Receptive field buildup comparison. ECG-AI uses six blocks with k=3 and strided convolutions at stage transitions. RepNet Baseline uses two blocks with wider kernels (k=7, k=5). Each block contains two convolutions followed by max-pooling.}
\label{tab:receptive-field}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{ECG-AI RF} & \textbf{RepNet Baseline RF} \\
\midrule
Input & 1 & 1 \\
Block 1: 2$\times$Conv + Pool & 8 & 14 \\
Block 2: 2$\times$Conv + Pool & 18 & 32 \\
Block 3: 2$\times$Conv(s=2) + Pool & 38 & --- \\
Block 4: 2$\times$Conv + Pool & 78 & --- \\
Block 5: 2$\times$Conv(s=2) + Pool & 158 & --- \\
Block 6: 2$\times$Conv + Pool & 318 & --- \\
\midrule
\textbf{Final conv RF (samples)} & \textbf{318} & \textbf{32} \\
\textbf{Final conv RF (seconds)} & \textbf{1.27\,s} & \textbf{0.13\,s} \\
\textbf{Exceeds 250 samples?} & Yes & No (+ GAP) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

ECG-AI reaches a receptive field of 318 samples (1.27\,s) through six blocks of small k=3 kernels with strided convolutions at stage transitions, comfortably exceeding the 250-sample threshold. RepNet Baseline reaches only 32 samples (0.13\,s) with two blocks --- far short of the 250-sample target for capturing the 1\,Hz discriminative feature. However, the global average pooling (GAP) at the fusion stage collapses the entire remaining temporal dimension, giving the classifier access to information across the full 2500-sample (10\,s) input. This design is deliberate: RepNet uses far fewer convolutional layers to minimize the parameter count --- critical given our dataset of 337 preeclampsia cases compared to Butler et al.'s larger multi-site cohort --- and delegates full-sequence context to the pooling and fusion stages rather than asking convolution alone to build sufficient receptive field.

\section{Optuna Hyperparameter Optimization}
To achieve optimal model performance with the CNN Transformer we will use ECG-AI as a baseline architecture and experiment with additional layers and hyperparameters using the Optuna framework. This requires making modular components — models with exposed hyperparameter inputs and interchangeable preprocessing and data augmentation pipelines supporting majority undersampling, SMOTE, or GAN strategies.

Similarly with random forest, we will tune all relevant parameters such as number of estimators, max features, and max depth. We will perform recursive feature elimination (RFE) to optimize the model input into the relevant features and see which are indiciative of preeclampsia \cite{joshi_linking_2025}. All intermediate weights, parameters, and performance metrics will be logged so that batch training jobs can yield both the most performant model and visualizations to bolster our research presentation.

\section{Ablation and Dimensionality Reduction}

We will analyze dimensionality reduction of our inputs, determining which leads are relevant for diagnosis of preeclampsia, for compute and resource constrained environments such as the developing world where preeclampsia disproportionately kills and could benefit from the most modest intervention. Such would enable using simpler non 12-lead ECG systems, and diagnosis from local and embedded compute on the spot rather than needing external compute clusters on premisis or in the cloud.

We will perform dimensionality reduction using principal component analysis (PCA) on both waveforms and features to determine the data with the most variance that may contribute most to classification, and experiment with models set only to have inputs from them, and furthermore only to train on them.

We will also do ablation study, where we selectively add or remove information such as masking certain leads, or injecting noise, to see what actually breaks the model versus leaves it unchanged. At the extreme we can do a combinatorial study of every possible subset of the 12-lead ECG, 2 to the power of 12 being 4096 sets, if compute constraints are not an issue such as cloud computing \cite{xue_does_2024}.

Lastly we may train an autoencoder such across leads to learn a compressed latent representation, and by varying bottleneck size we can see performance vs information capacity, seeing how much information we can compress and still retain performance, showing us how much information we can discard or start with in the first place. This would essentially be a continous version of channel dropout.


\section{Caliper Libtorch Visualization Utility}
The development of our model will ship alongside Caliper, a framework built atop of C++, Libtorch (Pytorch C++ API), and OpenGL, ImGUI and ImPLOT. It will be publicly available through github. It is a highly performant machine learning visualization tool able to display data elements, neural network layers, outputs in real time, and tie them to arbitrary rendering through whatever is possible with a 3D graphics API, and also offering a user interface to alter settings on the fly.

Development of the basic framework has been sucessful, with CUDA augmentation on Windows and MPS augmentation on MacOS. We will begin development of the graphical user interface for demonstration alongside model development. We are planning to have options to load ECG datasets in the format of CSV and then options to load models from standard PyTorch weight formats that can then automatically construct visualizations that can also show data propogation through the network.

For this research project specifically, we will have  ECG waveforms be displayed and show the translation of convolutional kernels and the attention map highlighting regions of interest for classification, and alongside that have the random forest classifier and its extraction of manual features be indicated so that there is explainability for the classification. We can showcase that a clinician can point to a heatmap and also state that a certain heartbeat feature has deviated from a healthy range.

Though caliper can implement training, it is not intended for this project as our workflow needs to be universally deployable in Python, with libraries that are not available in C++. But for much more intensive and performant projects caliper could be utilized. We intend to use Caliper to demonstrate our model's features at our tabling in the Davis Senior Design Showcase in June 2026, providing attendees with an opportunity to interact with our research. An example of what the Caliper interface could look like is shown in Appendix~\ref{fig:caliper-interface}.

\section{Conclusion}
We have completed a comprehensive review of the literature spanning preeclampsia ML, ECG classification, transformer architectures, foundation models, data augmentation, and model efficiency. We have identified that ECG waveform classification for preeclampsia is a largely untapped field with only one prior deep learning study by Butler et al. We have obtained and decomposed the Butler et al. ECG-AI model architecture which will serve as our baseline.

On the random forest side, we have implemented ECG featurization using Neurokit2 and validated our implementation against existing work. We are prepared to begin training once the UCDH Echo dataset is released, and will evaluate feature importance to determine which ECG features are most indicative of preeclampsia.

Our next steps are to begin model development using Optuna for hyperparameter optimization, experimenting with transformer augmented CNN architectures informed by the literature. We will conduct ablation studies and dimensionality reduction experiments to determine the minimum lead configuration necessary for performant classification. Data augmentation via SMOTE or transformer GAN will be explored to address class imbalance. Development of the Caliper visualization utility will continue in parallel to provide real time explainability for our models at the Davis Senior Design Showcase in June 2026.
