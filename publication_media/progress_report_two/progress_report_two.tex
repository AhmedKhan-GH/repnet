\section{Introduction}
Risk Estimation of Preeclampsia Network (RepNet) is our research in improving the machine learning and deep learning classification of 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) waveforms for estimation of preeclampsia. We implemented a novel convolutional neural network (CNN) with channel-independent 1-dimensional convolutional heads and an attention mechanism for cross-lead analysis.

This study builds on the sole previous study in CNN-based ECG classification for preeclampsia by Butler et al.~\cite{butler_ai-based_2024}. Furthermore, we implemented a random forest classifier that extracts manually specified ECG features to explain classification results. Both of these approaches build on general ideas and approaches from a previous study in ECG classification for RWMA by Joshi et al.~\cite{joshi_linking_2025}.

We have completed model training, data pre-processing, and augmentation strategy experimentation using Pytorch and Scikit-Learn running on the Optuna hyper-parameter optimization framework and have achieved promising results to be covered in this paper. Optuna uses Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) to maximize training results across runs by selecting hyperparameters from a continually updated probability distribution~\cite{akiba_optuna_2019}.

We have analyzed saliency maps, activation propagation, and attention maps to determine and show that the model does consistently recognize heartbeat intervals and focuses on particular ECG leads cited in preeclampsia literature~\cite{aboshady_qtc_2025}. We also observe a dimensionality reduction decision boundary that seems to be effective for certain cases but struggles with others, meaning there may be more preeclampsia morphologies to capture.

We intend to perform model compression for deployment on edge devices and compute-constrained environments such as Jetson Nano or Raspberry Pi.
Lastly, we intend to combine our model with our in-house real-time machine learning visualizer named Caliper to demonstrate clinical workflows augmented by machine learning and deep learning, for final presentation at the UC Davis Senior Design Showcase in June 2026.

\subsection{Channel Independent -- Shared Weights}

RepNet processes each lead through its own 1D convolutional stream with no cross-lead interaction until the attention step (\textit{channel-independent}). The question: does preeclampsia alter \textit{how leads relate to each other}, or \textit{what each lead looks like individually}?

We compute the $12 \times 12$ Pearson correlation matrix $C^{(n)}$ across leads for each recording $n$:
\begin{equation}
C^{(n)}_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl(x^{(n)}_{i,t} - \bar{x}^{(n)}_i\bigr)\bigl(x^{(n)}_{j,t} - \bar{x}^{(n)}_j\bigr)}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(x^{(n)}_{i,t} - \bar{x}^{(n)}_i\bigr)^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl(x^{(n)}_{j,t} - \bar{x}^{(n)}_j\bigr)^2}}
\end{equation}
where $x^{(n)}_{i,t}$ is the voltage of lead $i$ at time $t$. Averaging within each class:
\begin{equation}
\bar{C}_{\text{PreE}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{PreE}}} \sum_{n \in \text{PreE}} C^{(n)}, \qquad
\bar{C}_{\text{Normal}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{Normal}}} \sum_{n \in \text{Normal}} C^{(n)}
\end{equation}

If preeclampsia disrupts inter-lead relationships, $\bar{C}_{\text{PreE}} - \bar{C}_{\text{Normal}}$ would show large off-diagonal entries, favoring 2D convolution across leads. We quantify this with the Frobenius norm:
\begin{equation}
\| \bar{C}_{\text{PreE}} - \bar{C}_{\text{Normal}} \|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{12}\sum_{j=1}^{12} \left(\bar{C}_{\text{PreE},ij} - \bar{C}_{\text{Normal},ij}\right)^2} = 0.981
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{frobenius_diff.png}
\caption{Inter-lead correlation matrices for Normal ($N$\,=\,1843) and PreE ($N$\,=\,335) classes, and their difference.}
\label{fig:frobenius}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:frobenius}, the two matrices are nearly identical. The difference matrix has maximum entries below $\pm 0.18$, and the Frobenius norm of 0.981 is well below the 2.0 threshold for clinically meaningful disruption. The discriminative signal is within each lead's waveform (ST/T-wave morphology), not in inter-lead coupling. This justifies per-lead 1D convolution.

RepNet uses shared weights: one CNN applied identically to every lead, rather than 12 independent CNNs. Two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Shared structure.} All 12 leads record the same heartbeat, differing only in amplitude/polarity due to the cardiac axis. The same convolutional filters are useful across all leads.
\item \textbf{Data multiplication.} With 335 PreE recordings, independent weights per lead see 335 examples each. Shared weights see $335 \times 12 = 4{,}020$ lead-level examples --- critical regularization in a data-limited regime.
\end{enumerate}

The trade-off --- shared weights cannot learn lead-specific features --- is handled by the cross-lead attention mechanism, which learns to weight leads differently after per-lead features are extracted.

\subsection{Three-Stage Progressive Abstraction}

RepNet processes each lead through three convolutional stages with doubling filter counts (48$\to$96$\to$192) and shrinking kernel sizes (7$\to$5$\to$3). MaxPool(2) halves the temporal axis at each stage: $2500 \to 1250 \to 625 \to 312$ samples. Table~\ref{tab:arch} summarizes the architecture.

The design rationale is frequency-driven. Wider kernels at early stages capture broad morphological features (QRS complex) on raw 250\,Hz signals. Narrower kernels at deeper stages refine on already-downsampled representations where each sample spans a larger temporal window. Each PerLeadConvBlock contains two same-padded convolutions followed by MaxPool(2). Treating the pool as a stride-only operation, the receptive field at stage $s$ accumulates as:
\begin{equation}
\text{RF}_s = \text{RF}_{s-1} + 2\,(k_s - 1) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{s-1} 2
\end{equation}
where the factor of 2 inside the product is the pool stride per stage. Expanding for three stages:
\begin{align}
\text{RF} &= 1 + 2(k_1{-}1)\cdot 1 + 2(k_2{-}1)\cdot 2 + 2(k_3{-}1)\cdot 4 \notag \\
           &= 2k_1 + 4k_2 + 8k_3 - 13 \notag \\
           &= 2(7) + 4(5) + 8(3) - 13 = 45 \text{ samples} = 180\text{\,ms}
\end{align}
This covers the QRS complex (80--120\,ms) and extends into the ST segment, where preeclampsia's primary ECG signature --- mild repolarization abnormalities --- resides. The GAP layer in the fusion head subsequently collapses the full remaining temporal dimension, giving the classifier access to the entire 10\,s recording.

\subsection{Cross-Lead Attention Mechanism}

After each per-lead convolutional stage, a cross-lead attention block allows the 12 leads to exchange information. The mechanism operates as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Tokenization.} Each lead's feature map $(C \times T)$ is pooled to a single $C$-dimensional vector via AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1), yielding 12 tokens.
\item \textbf{Self-attention.} 4-head multi-head attention operates over the 12 lead-tokens with residual connection and LayerNorm.
\item \textbf{Gating.} The attended representation passes through a linear layer and sigmoid to produce per-lead gate weights in $[0, 1]$. These weights are broadcast back to the full temporal resolution and applied element-wise: $\mathbf{x}_\ell' = \sigma(\mathbf{g}_\ell) \odot \mathbf{x}_\ell$.
\end{enumerate}

The gating design is deliberate. Rather than replacing each lead's features with the attention output, the gate \textit{modulates} the original full-resolution features. Leads deemed uninformative by attention are suppressed; informative leads are amplified. This preserves temporal detail that would be lost if the pooled attention output were used directly.

Attention is placed between every convolutional stage (three attention blocks total), not only at the end. This allows cross-lead context to inform feature extraction at each abstraction level: QRS-level (Stage~1), ST-level (Stage~2), and morphology-level (Stage~3).

Compared to 2D convolution across leads, attention preserves lead identity throughout the network and produces interpretable per-lead weights that can be analyzed post hoc to determine which leads the model relies on for classification. Figure~\ref{fig:attention} shows the mean $12 \times 12$ attention matrices across the test set, separated by class.

Stage~1 attention is nearly perfectly uniform (deviations $< 0.1\%$ from $1/12$), confirming that early-stage features are not yet class-discriminative. Stages~2 and~3 develop \textit{vertical striping}: entire columns are consistently brighter or darker regardless of the query row. This key-dominated pattern means the model has learned \textit{which leads are worth listening to} rather than pairwise lead relationships --- every lead looks to the same informative sources.

The column structure is consistent across both classes: aVR and V1 are suppressed ($\sim$0.6--0.9\% below uniform), while lateral leads V5, V6, II, and aVF are boosted ($\sim$0.3--0.4\% above uniform). This aligns with clinical expectations --- aVR is an inverted view of the heart that rarely carries primary diagnostic information, while the lateral and inferior leads are most sensitive to the repolarization abnormalities associated with preeclampsia. Class-level differences are subtle ($<$0.03\% of uniform) but structured: in Stage~2, PreE samples attend more to aVL and Lead~I (the high-lateral leads) and less to V2--V3, consistent with left-axis-directed repolarization changes. These small attention shifts are amplified by the multiplicative sigmoid gate acting on full-resolution feature maps.

\subsection{Fusion Head and Classification}

The fusion head is the only component where all 12 leads merge into a single representation. After Stage~3, the 12 lead feature maps are concatenated along the channel axis: $(B, 12, 192, 312) \to (B, 2304, 312)$. A pointwise $1
\times 1$ convolution reduces this to $(B, 192, 312)$ with BatchNorm and ReLU, forcing the network to learn a compact cross-lead representation rather than retaining all $12 \times 192 = 2304$ channels. Global average pooling (GAP) then collapses the temporal axis to a 192-dimensional vector. Dropout followed by a fully-connected layer produces the 2-class output (PreE vs.\ Normal).

The 1x1 convolution is preferred over flattening for parameter efficiency: flattening $2304 \times 312 = 718$K activations directly into a two-class FC layer would exceed the entire model's parameter budget. The pointwise conv uses only $2304 \times 192 = 442$K parameters and preserves the temporal structure for GAP.

The final classification reduces to a 192-channel vote. Each GAP channel $i$ contributes a vote $v_i = \text{GAP}_i \cdot (W_{\text{PreE},i} - W_{\text{Normal},i})$ to the PreE-vs-Normal decision margin; the sum of all votes plus a bias term passes through softmax. Figure~\ref{fig:voting} visualizes this mechanism. The structural weights (top) show which channels are wired toward PreE (positive) or Normal (negative). The per-sample panels show a confident true positive (margin $= +1.24$, PreE votes dominate) and a confident true negative (margin $= -4.28$, Normal votes dominate).

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}

Three preprocessing steps are applied in order:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Baseline wander removal.} A 4th-order Butterworth high-pass filter at 0.5\,Hz, the clinical standard cutoff for ECG baseline correction~\cite{jia_preprocessing_2024}, removes slow electrode drift. Implemented in SOS (second-order sections) form for numerical stability.
\item \textbf{Powerline interference removal.} A notch filter at 60\,Hz ($Q = 30$) suppresses US power grid interference. The narrow $Q$ factor limits the notch width to $\sim$1\,Hz, preserving signal content near 60\,Hz.
\item \textbf{Z-score normalization.} Each lead is independently normalized to zero mean and unit variance: $(x - \mu_\ell) / (\sigma_\ell + \epsilon)$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. Per-lead normalization preserves amplitude differences between leads that encode cardiac axis information.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Split and Balancing}
\label{sec:data-split}

An 80/20 patient-grouped holdout split ensures no patient appears in both dev and test sets. The dev set contains 1{,}747 recordings (15.9\% PreE); the test set contains 431 recordings (13.5\% PreE). The test set is never balanced, preserving clinical prevalence for evaluation.

Within the dev set, training data is balanced via symmetric augmentation followed by majority undersampling. Both PreE and Normal samples are augmented with Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.06$) and random time shifts (up to $\pm 276$ samples $\approx \pm 1.1$\,s), doubling each class. Majority undersampling then reduces the Normal class to match PreE count, yielding a 1:1 training ratio. Because the Normal class is undersampled after doubling, the final training set contains a fair mix of original and augmented Normal waveforms rather than being entirely synthetic. Augmenting both classes symmetrically prevents the model from learning augmentation artifacts as class-discriminative features.

\section{Model Analysis}

The 192-dimensional GAP embedding produced by RepNet's penultimate layer provides a rich space for post-hoc analysis of what the model has learned, where it fails, and how its decision landscape is structured. All analyses in this section use the best single seed (seed \#49, test AUROC = 0.821) with PCA fit on the development set to avoid leakage.

\subsection{Embedding Geometry}

Dimensionality reduction of the 192-d GAP embeddings confirms that the ECG carries a learnable preeclampsia signal. We apply two complementary projections. PCA (principal component analysis) finds the linear axes of greatest variance, preserving global structure. t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) is a nonlinear method that optimizes a probabilistic mapping to preserve local neighborhood structure --- points close in 192-d stay close in 2-d, revealing clusters and manifold geometry that linear projections miss.

Both projections (Figures~\ref{fig:pca_class}--\ref{fig:tsne_prob}) show PreE samples concentrating in a distinct region of embedding space, separated from the bulk Normal population. The model's predicted $P(\text{PreE})$ tracks this geometry closely: high-confidence predictions (red) align with the PreE cluster while low-confidence predictions (blue) align with the Normal cluster, producing a visible decision boundary with a gradient transition zone where the two classes overlap. The t-SNE projection reveals tighter cluster structure than PCA, with a geometrically distinct high-confidence Normal cluster that is not simply a linear tail of the distribution.

The representation is also highly compressed. The first 5 principal components capture 87.8\% of variance, and the channel correlation matrix has an effective rank of 6.0 --- the 192-channel GAP layer encodes approximately 6 independent dimensions of variation. A linear probe on just 7 PCs achieves comparable test AUROC to the full nonlinear model (0.826 vs.\ 0.821), indicating that the discriminative signal is low-dimensional and approximately linear in the learned feature space.

\section{Model Interpretability}

To understand what the model has learned beyond aggregate metrics, we examine two complementary views of a single confident true positive ($P(\text{PreE}){=}0.965$): gradient-based saliency maps that highlight \textit{which input regions} drive the prediction, and layer-by-layer activation propagation that shows \textit{how the signal transforms} through the network.

\subsection{Saliency Maps}

We compute a hybrid attribution by averaging normalized Integrated Gradients (IG) and Grad-CAM, then log-scaling to preserve detail in low-magnitude regions. IG integrates gradients along an interpolation path from a zero baseline to the input, producing per-sample per-lead attributions. Grad-CAM extracts class-discriminative spatial maps from the final convolutional stage. The combination captures both fine-grained input sensitivity (IG) and coarse spatial localization (Grad-CAM).

Figure~\ref{fig:saliency} shows all 12 leads ranked by total attribution magnitude. Red regions indicate features that push the prediction toward PreE; blue regions push toward Normal. The model consistently highlights R-peak onsets and the ST--T segment across multiple heartbeat cycles. Leads V6 and V3 show the strongest attributions, with the ST segment and T-wave morphology receiving the most attention.

\subsection{Clinical Significance of Saliency Patterns}

Preeclampsia causes QTc interval prolongation of 21.7\,ms compared to normal pregnancy~\cite{aboshady_qtc_2025}, reflecting altered ventricular repolarization driven by endothelial dysfunction and increased systemic vascular resistance~\cite{raffaelli_pre-eclampsia_2014}. These hemodynamic changes manifest on the ECG primarily in the ST segment and T-wave --- the electrical signature of ventricular repolarization~\cite{angeli_electrocardiographic_2014}.

The saliency maps confirm that the model has learned to localize to these clinically relevant features. In the top-ranked leads (V6, V3, aVL), the strongest PreE-favoring (red) regions consistently align with the ST segment and T-wave descending limb --- precisely the interval where QTc prolongation stretches and flattens the waveform morphology. The attribution repeats periodically across all visible cardiac cycles, indicating the model aggregates evidence from multiple heartbeats.

The Normal-favoring (blue) regions are equally informative: they cluster around the TP baseline between T-wave offset and the next P-wave onset, where a healthy ECG should be electrically silent. A clean isoelectric return is itself evidence against repolarization abnormality, and the model has learned to weight this absence of pathology toward Normal classification.

\subsection{Activation Propagation}

Figure~\ref{fig:act_perlead} traces Lead~II's representation through all three convolutional stages. At each stage, the conv output ($C \times T_s$ feature map) and the attention-gated output are shown below the raw input waveform.

In Stage~1 (48 channels $\times$ 1250 timesteps), individual channels develop clear heartbeat-locked activation patterns --- sparse hot spots aligned with QRS complexes and T-waves, confirming that the first convolutional block learns basic morphological detectors. The attention-gated output is visually similar, consistent with the near-uniform Stage~1 attention weights observed in Section~4. Stage~2 (96 channels $\times$ 625 timesteps) shows increased channel specialization: some channels respond selectively to R-peaks while others capture inter-beat intervals. Stage~3 (192 channels $\times$ 312 timesteps) produces the most abstract representation, with diffuse activations that encode longer-range temporal patterns rather than individual waveform features.

The progressive abstraction is visible in the activation density. Stage~1 feature maps are sparse --- most channels are near zero except at heartbeat events, indicating the early filters act as edge detectors for the QRS complex. By Stage~3, activations are denser and more distributed across the temporal axis, reflecting that deeper layers encode context-dependent representations that integrate information across multiple heartbeat intervals. This transition from local morphological detection to global temporal encoding is consistent with the receptive field analysis in Section~2: Stage~1 blocks have a 52\,ms receptive field (sub-QRS), while the cumulative receptive field through Stage~3 spans 180\,ms, covering the full QRS--ST interval.

\subsection{Implementation and Results}

The Python library Neurokit has methods for cleaning ECG signals along with extracting certain features. Through the use of these methods, we are able to implement the features in Table~\ref{tab:ecg-features}. Hana Shaik, our graduate mentor, has already implemented ST-T abnormalities and used these features in detecting RWMA. Testing occurred on the Nightingale ECG dataset to ensure that proper featurization was occurring. As another form of validation, we were able to compare our implementation to Hana's implementation. After receiving the balanced UCDH Echo dataset, we reformatted our Nightingale code to be able to process individual CSV ECG files instead of individual lead files.

The main issue as of the moment is figuring out accurate QRS axis and T-axis calculations in order to also obtain the frontal QRS-T angle. Currently we have two implementations for the QRS axis. The first is the isoelectric method, which estimates the axis by pinpointing the isoelectric lead and going $\pm 90^{\circ}$ based on whether the perpendicular pair has a positive or negative QRS value. The second uses the equation $\text{QRS-axis} = \pm \arctan\!\left(\frac{2 \cdot \text{aVF}}{\sqrt{3} \cdot \text{I}}\right)$, which gives a specific numeric value, though in clinical practice small degree differences are insignificant. Dr.\ Ebong, a UC Davis Health cardiologist, is currently evaluating the accuracy of our implementations.

Before training a random forest model on our datasets, it is beneficial to extract novel MUSE features that correlate with the Neurokit features to determine if Neurokit performs well. Specific ECGs were dropped in order to obtain Neurokit featurizations for both balanced and unbalanced ECG waveform datasets with the detailed specifics in Table~\ref{tab:rf-dataset}. In order to stay consistent, the MUSE featurization also drops the same ECGs and compares only features that can be extracted from their respective metadata CSVs. This results in not being able to compare the amplitude differential features in model performance due to the inability to obtain the PR amplitudes from the metadata CSVs. Table~\ref{tab:muse-features} lists the MUSE feature implementations. After observing the average percent differences between the Neurokit and MUSE features, Neurokit feature values were consistently lower in magnitude than MUSE.

Feature engineering is the next step to obtaining a better model. \section{Discussion}

The RepNet CrossLead architecture achieves a best-seed AUROC of 0.821 on the held-out test set, which is notable given the relatively small dataset of 2,178 recordings and the difficulty of detecting a subtle cardiac phenotype from standard 12-lead ECGs. The multi-seed validation (mean AUROC $0.744 \pm 0.053$ across 30 seeds) reveals meaningful variance, suggesting sensitivity to train/test partitioning that larger datasets would stabilize. These figures compare favorably to Butler et al.'s AUC of 0.85 on a larger cohort~\cite{butler_ai-based_2024}, particularly given our stricter patient-grouped splitting to prevent data leakage.

Interpretability analyses provide converging evidence that the model captures clinically meaningful patterns. Saliency maps highlight ST-segment and T-wave regions across precordial leads --- the intervals where preeclampsia-induced QTc prolongation and repolarization abnormalities manifest~\cite{aboshady_qtc_2025, angeli_electrocardiographic_2014}. The random forest corroborates these findings: chi-squared feature importance identifies aVL ST depression, V4 ST depression, and V3 ST elevation among the most discriminative features, and the decision tree splits on ST amplitude differentials and $T_{pe}$/QT ratios. This convergence between a black-box CNN and an interpretable feature-based classifier strengthens the clinical plausibility of the identified ECG biomarkers.

Several limitations remain. The 5.5:1 class imbalance challenges sensitivity despite cost-weighted training. The single-site UC Davis Health dataset may limit generalizability. The Neurokit featurization pipeline currently lacks validated QRS axis and T-axis implementations, leaving the frontal QRS-T angle unexplored in the RF model.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented RepNet, a channel-independent CNN with cross-lead attention for preeclampsia detection from 12-lead ECGs, achieving a best AUROC of 0.821 on a patient-grouped held-out test set of 431 recordings from the UC Davis Health dataset. Multi-seed validation across 30 random seeds yields a mean AUROC of $0.744 \pm 0.053$, establishing a robust performance baseline. Saliency mapping and activation propagation confirm that the model focuses on ST-segment and T-wave intervals in precordial leads, consistent with the QTc prolongation and repolarization abnormalities documented in preeclampsia literature. A parallel random forest classifier using manually extracted ECG features corroborates these findings, with chi-squared feature importance highlighting ST amplitude differentials on the same leads.

Future work will focus on: (1)~expanding the dataset through multi-site collaboration and SMOTE-based augmentation to address class imbalance and improve generalizability, (2)~completing the RF feature pipeline with validated QRS axis calculations and recursive feature elimination to identify a minimal discriminative feature set, and (3)~performing ablation studies to determine the minimum lead configuration necessary for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. In parallel, we are developing Caliper, a real-time machine learning visualization utility built on C++, LibTorch, and OpenGL. Caliper will provide interactive visualization of ECG waveforms, convolutional activation maps, attention heatmaps, and random forest feature extractions, enabling clinicians to inspect both the deep learning and feature-based explanations for a given classification. We intend to demonstrate Caliper alongside our models at the UC Davis Senior Design Showcase in June 2026.
